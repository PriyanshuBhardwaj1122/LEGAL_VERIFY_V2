# The Checkbox That Passes for Consent: Why India's DPDP Regime Cannot Deliver on Its Promise to Children

## A US time-limit, a Meta announcement, and a statute that never asked for proof

Section 9 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 conditions all processing of a child's personal data on "verified parental consent" and forbids "tracking, behavioural monitoring, or targeted advertising of children of any kind"  ("Legal Protection of Children's Data under India's Digital", ijassonline.in). No rules operationalising that verification standard have been framed on this record; what exists is the statutory text and a legislative history — from the 2019 Bill's requirement of "age verification and parental consent mechanisms" through the 2023 Bill's simpler rule that consent "will be provided by the parent or the legal guardian"  ("The Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019", PRS Legislative Research)  ("The Digital Personal Data Protection Bill, 2023", PRS Legislative Research) — and that history shows the verification requirement itself was diluted before enactment, not merely left to subordinate rulemaking.

Three other regimes handle the same problem differently. Apple has made a "Child Account" mandatory for under-13 users, binding age status to device-level controls rather than to a form filled out once  ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com). US state minor-privacy statutes impose obligations keyed to whether a service is "directed at minors" or "collects personal information about known minors," a standard built on platform-side inference about the audience and the data, not on what a user typed into a birthdate field  ("Data protection laws in India", dlapiperdataprotection.com). By contrast, India's statute asks for none of this: consent comes from "the parent or the legal guardian"  ("The Digital Personal Data Protection Bill, 2023", PRS Legislative Research), and nothing in the text requires the platform to verify that the adult clicking is a parent, or that the child's declared age is true.

The uncomfortable fact is that a law advertised as importing GDPR-style protection retains a blanket age of 18 while, on one commentator's account, "under-regulating actual data practices"  ("Legal Protection of Children's Data under India's Digital", ijassonline.in). It is stringent on paper and unverifiable in practice. This paper grants that uniform India-wide regulation is a live administrative concern, and that European-style age-gating risks excluding exactly the children it protects; it nonetheless concludes that Section 9, absent any verification mechanism, cannot deliver on 9(3)'s promise. The sections below trace why identity-proofing was dropped, how platforms exploit checkbox consent, and what verification model could still be retrofitted.

## Section 9 was built for a real problem, and self-declaration was a defensible compromise in 2023

The self-declaration model in Section 9 deserves a fairer hearing than it usually gets. The statutory design is not, on its own terms, arbitrary: it requires "verifiable parent or legal guardian" consent that is "free, specific, informed, unambiguous, and based on clear affirmative action" (Section 9 of the DPDP Act, Section 9), and it backs this with a prohibition on tracking, profiling, behavioural monitoring, or targeted advertising directed at children, enforceable by penalties running up to ₹250 crores (Section 9(3) of the DPDP Act, Section 9(3)) ("Legal Protection of Children's Data under India's Digital", ijassonline.in). A regime built around those two pillars — parental gatekeeping plus a hard behavioural prohibition — is a coherent legislative strategy, not a placeholder.

The harder question is what "verifiable" could plausibly have meant in 2023. Meaningful age or identity verification, as MediaNama's founder points out, has an unavoidable structural cost: "to verify who's a child, they'll have to verify everyone who's using a service" ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com). Once that infrastructure exists for children, it becomes a general surveillance layer for adults too, and a drafter facing that choice in 2023 had no ready alternative. There was no operational national identity rail that private platforms could call on to confirm a user's age without onboarding every user into a verification system, and mandating that children themselves submit government identity documents to commercial platforms raises the same privacy and proportionality concerns the DPDP framework was built to address. Self-declaration, paired with parental consent and a blanket behavioural prohibition, was the compromise available. It avoided building a universal identity-verification apparatus while still placing legal responsibility on platforms not to track or profile minors. None of this excuses what came after. Nikhil Pahwa's charge that self-declaration "makes the law redundant because children will lie about their age" ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com) remains a serious criticism of the Rules that operationalised Section 9, but it is a criticism of implementation choices made after 2023, not of the original statutory judgment. That judgment was defensible only so long as no usable, privacy-respecting verification rail existed for private platforms to rely on; it is that absence, not any principle in Section 9 itself, that has since been overtaken by DigiLocker and Aadhaar-linked verification infrastructure.

## No Indian court has yet been asked whether a time-cap is a restriction on speech or a protection of the child

The question this article's title poses — whether a time-cap on a child's social media use is a restriction on speech or a protection of the child — has not been put to any Indian court. The record shows why that gap matters rather than being a mere curiosity. The nearest doctrinal materials lie in child-welfare statutes wholly outside the social-media context: the Juvenile Justice Act recognises "a right to protection of his privacy and confidentiality, by all means and throughout the judicial process"  (THE JUVENILE JUSTICE (CARE AND PROTECTION OF CHILDREN) ACT, 2015, s. 3(xi)), and vests in the Child Welfare Committee a power to determine a guardian's "suitability" before restoring a child to their care  (THE JUVENILE JUSTICE (CARE AND PROTECTION OF ...). These are holdings about custody, rehabilitation and confidentiality in judicial proceedings — a register entirely different from screen-time or access-hour limits imposed on a platform, and no court has read across from this jurisprudence to ask whether capping a minor's hours on an app engages Article 19(1)(a) as a restriction on expression, or Article 21 as a protective measure, or both at once.

Scholarship has tried to build the constitutional vocabulary such a case would need, arguing that "children's digital identity should be recognized as an independent constitutional interest deserving heightened protection under Article 21"  ("Constitutional Protection of Children's Digital Identity and Data", lawjournals.celnet.in) and proposing a "child-centric constitutional framework for digital governance"  ("Constitutional Protection of Children's Digital Identity and Data", lawjournals.celnet.in). That these proposals exist only in law review pages, not in any reported judgment, is itself the point: commentary can gesture at how a court might resolve the speech-versus-protection question, but it cannot substitute for a holding. Even the National Human Rights Commission's own discussion of regulating children's social media access proceeded through an open house, not adjudication. Its Chairperson called merely for "enforceable and practical solutions"  ("NHRC, National Human Rights Commission India, nhrc", nhrc.nic.in), and a member favoured regulation over prohibition  ("NHRC, National Human Rights Commission India, nhrc", nhrc.nic.in) — administrative advocacy, not binding law.

The consequence follows directly for the DPDP Act's credibility. Because no court has tested time or access restrictions against Article 19 or 21, any claim that such measures are "legal" in India is an inference from adjacent doctrine, not a holding — which matters precisely because Section 9's consent architecture already rests on self-declaration rather than verification, leaving its protective claims equally untested against the constitutional standard commentators say ought to apply.

## Section 9(3)'s ban on tracking survives only until a platform decides consent has been obtained

Section 9(3) prohibits "tracking, profiling, behavioural monitoring, or targeted advertising directed at children"  (Section 9(3) of the DPDP Act, Section 9(3)), and on its face this reads as a substantive rule independent of the consent mechanism in Section 9(1). But the two provisions share a single predicate: both apply only once a platform has decided a user is a child, and that determination rests on consent obtained from "a verifiable parent or legal guardian" that must be "free, specific, informed, unambiguous, and based on clear affirmative action"  (Section 9 of the DPDP Act, Section 9). Nothing in the statutory scheme separates the gate that triggers Section 9(3)'s ban from the gate that discharges Section 9(1)'s consent duty. They are the same gate.

This matters because of how that gate is actually built. Age itself is self-declared, a design choice Nikhil Pahwa has called an effective "bypass" that "makes the law redundant because Children will lie about their age"  ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com). If age is self-declared, then whether a user counts as a "child" for Section 9(3) purposes is itself unverified — meaning the tracking prohibition activates (or fails to activate) on the same unaudited assertion that satisfies the parental-consent requirement. A platform that collects one checkbox — an assertion of age, paired with a claimed parental consent — has simultaneously discharged its Section 9(1) obligation and defined itself out of, or into, Section 9(3)'s prohibition. Commentary describing the Act as relying on parents who "lack the awareness or technical knowledge to make nuanced privacy decisions"  ("Governing Learner Data Risks in India: The DPDP Act and the Case for EdTech-Specific Regulation", orfonline.org) applies with equal force here: a parent unable to evaluate consent terms is equally unable to police whether the platform, having obtained that consent, then refrains from behavioural monitoring.

One might object that Section 9(3) is a conduct rule, not a consent rule, and so should stand independently of how consent was obtained. That would matter if the Act made verification, rather than declaration, the trigger for either provision — it does not. The consequence is that the Act does not create two independent lines of defence for children's data, a consent line and a conduct line. It creates one line, and asks a single unverifiable checkbox to hold it. Where that checkbox fails — through a lied-about age or a parent's uninformed click — both the consent requirement and the tracking ban fail together, not sequentially. The protection promised by Section 9(3) is therefore no sturdier than the proof it is built on, and that proof, on the Act's own terms, is nothing more than an assertion nobody checks.

## The popular objection — 'Meta is voluntarily doing more than the law requires' — mistakes a business decision for legal compliance

A recurring move in the commentary treats Meta's own product features — age-signal prompts, default privacy settings for minors, parental controls of the kind Apple has made mandatory for its under-13 "Child Account" ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com) — as proof that the DPDP regime is doing its job, on the theory that a company would not build beyond a low statutory floor unless the surrounding pressure, regulatory and reputational, made it worth doing. The argument has a surface plausibility: if platforms are voluntarily layering age-related controls on top of Section 9's bare checkbox consent, something must be pushing them past the legal minimum, and that something is offered as evidence the framework is "working."

The argument fails on its own terms once the source of that pressure is examined. The comparable feature at Apple was made mandatory by product design choice, not by an Indian statute. The wider wave of minor-specific obligations that companies are absorbing traces to a different jurisdiction's law altogether — a rapidly developing body of American state privacy statutes directed at services aimed at minors under eighteen ("Data protection laws in India", dlapiperdataprotection.com). A platform calibrating its global product to satisfy the strictest market it operates in is complying with that market's law, not India's. Nothing in Section 9 compels Meta to retain any particular feature once it stops being commercially convenient; the obligation the statute actually imposes is self-declared age plus parental checkbox consent, and nothing more. A voluntary feature installed for foreign-market reasons and withdrawable at will is not a regulatory outcome. It is a business decision that happens, for now, to point the same direction as the statute's stated purpose.

The NHRC's own posture is instructive on this point: its Open House on children's social media access was convened precisely because existing arrangements were judged insufficiently enforceable, with its Chairperson calling for practical, enforceable solutions and a uniform law rather than pointing to industry self-regulation as adequate ("NHRC, National Human Rights Commission India, nhrc", nhrc.nic.in) ("NHRC, National Human Rights Commission India, nhrc", nhrc.nic.in). If the body charged with examining this question is asking for enforceable rules, corporate goodwill cannot be the answer already achieved. The conclusion this section reaches is narrower than a claim about safety: whatever protection exists today runs on Meta's continued commercial preference, not on any obligation Section 9 makes it keep.

## Even a fully notified verification rule would not close the design gap

Suppose the self-declaration loophole is closed tomorrow: MeitY notifies a mandatory government-ID or third-party age-verification mechanism, and Meta can no longer rely on a child clicking "I am 18." Nikhil Pahwa's objection to self-declaration — that "self-declaration makes the law redundant because Children will lie about their age"  ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com) — would then have been answered. The question this section asks is whether answering it closes the gap the thesis identifies, or only moves it.

It does not close it, because Section 9's verification duty and Section 9(3)'s design duty are addressed to two different moments. Verification, however implemented, operates at the gate: it establishes who may enter and whose consent is required before processing begins  (Section 9 of the DPDP Act, Section 9). Section 9(3) operates after the gate, prohibiting "tracking, profiling, behavioural monitoring, or targeted advertising directed at children"  (Section 9(3) of the DPDP Act, Section 9(3)) in the ongoing conduct of the service. A verified thirteen-year-old, correctly identified and consented for by a parent, is still handed an interface built on infinite scroll, autoplay and notification-driven recommendation loops — the very engagement architecture Section 9(3) targets, and nothing in an identity check tells a platform how to design the feed once the child is inside it. This is visible in what the government's own preferred fix does: Apple's Child Account model restricts app access, communication and content filters at the account-configuration layer  ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com). That is a gatekeeping response, not a design-mandate response. It does not purport to touch recommendation logic at all.

The strongest objection is that verification and design are not really separable — that once a platform knows a user is a minor, it becomes accountable for what it builds for that user, and enforcement follows naturally. Yet this proves too much: MediaNama's founder observes that verifying children requires "verify everyone who's using a service"  ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com), which shows the verification apparatus solves an identification problem, not a design problem. It multiplies who is checked, not what is checked. Identity-proofing fixes the entry gate. Section 9(3) remains, on this reasoning, as unenforced as before.

## The strongest case for leaving verification out of the statute is real, and it does not rescue the regime

The strongest objection to this paper's argument does not come from anyone defending Section 9; it comes from those who would push further, toward mandatory verification, and are met by a warning worth taking seriously. As MediaNama's founder puts it, "to verify who's a child, they'll have to verify everyone who's using a service," and "once that verification mechanism comes in, it will be used for everything." ("Apple's New 'Child Account' Becomes Mandatory For Under-13s", medianama.com) The objection is not that self-declared age is too weak a check; it is that any check strong enough to matter would require collecting identity documents, biometric markers, or government-issued proof from the entire user base, adult and child alike, simply to isolate the minority who are minors. That is a real cost. Nor does it sit outside existing precedent — India's traceability mandate for messaging intermediaries already shows how a requirement aimed at a narrow harm can be built as a system capable of identifying anyone. ("The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and", PRS Legislative Research)

This should be conceded without qualification. A statute that solved under-verification by mandating national-ID-linked age checks would trade one failure for a worse one: a centralised, breach-prone map of which accounts belong to children, held by the same platforms whose business model this paper has already shown depends on engagement, not restraint. That risk is not speculative; it is the direct and foreseeable consequence of building verification the naive way.

Yet the objection proves less than it appears to. It is an argument against a particular design of verification — one keyed to persistent identity — not against verification as such. Age can be attested cryptographically, through a token or zero-knowledge proof that confirms a threshold without disclosing identity, age, or any linkable credential to the platform. The MediaNama warning is a reason to legislate that design carefully. It is not a reason to leave Section 9 where it stands, resting on a checkbox no regulator ever inspects.

## What the DPDP Rules would need to say for the thesis to fail

The industry position deserves to be stated in its strongest form before it is set aside. Self-declaration of age, on this view, is not an evasion of Section 9 but a reasonable interim measure, since verifying the age of every user would require collecting identity documents from the entire user base, adults included, trading one privacy harm for another. That argument has force, but it does not answer the narrower question this article has pressed throughout, which is not whether verification is easy but whether Section 9 currently requires it. It does not. A platform that collects a checkbox age declaration and a checkbox parental consent has, on the statute's own terms, satisfied Section 9 in full, whatever design choices it makes afterward under Section 9(3).

That is the finding this section reaches, and it is narrower than the surrounding critique might suggest: not that the Act is poorly drafted in general, but that it is presently silent on three specific points, each of which would need to be filled before the regime could be called protective rather than declaratory. First, a notified verification standard specifying what evidence of age or parental identity, if any, a fiduciary must obtain beyond self-declaration. Second, penalty data showing whether reliance on unverified self-declaration has in fact been treated as non-compliant by the authority, as distinct from the statute's bare penalty ceiling. Third, an audit mechanism capable of testing, at the design level, whether a platform's architecture actually complies with Section 9(3)'s prohibition on tracking and profiling. Absent all three, the statute regulates form, not conduct.
