# Eligibility Criteria under Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016: A Critical Examination

## Introduction: The Complex Landscape of Section 29A

Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) serves as a rigorous gatekeeper, disqualifying certain individuals from submitting resolution plans. It is designed to prevent those responsible for a debtor's financial distress from regaining control of the corporate debtor through insolvency proceedings  (Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. vs Union Of India, 2019, para 2). The Supreme Court has upheld the constitutional validity of Section 29A, emphasizing its role in safeguarding the resolution process's integrity by excluding persons deemed undesirable  (Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. vs Union Of India, 2019, para 5).

The ineligibility under Section 29A attaches at the time of submission of the resolution plan, ensuring that applicants are scrutinized at the critical juncture of proposing a resolution  (Company Appeal (AT) (Ins) No.121 of 2019, para 15). This provision has been interpreted to prevent "back-door entry to the promoter," thereby blocking those who could exploit the system to benefit from their own defaults  (IA No.30/2022 IN C.P. (IB)/349(ALD) 2018, para 8).

However, the application of Section 29A is not without its complexities. The NCLT, referencing the Supreme Court's interpretation, has highlighted challenges regarding the term "related party," and how it affects eligibility, noting that individuals acting jointly with others can be disqualified  (IA No.30/2022 IN C.P. (IB)/349(ALD) 2018, para 10). Furthermore, exemptions exist for Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs), which are not subject to certain disqualifications under Section 29A, reflecting legislative intent to support smaller businesses  (IA No.192 of 2022 in CP (IB) No.196/BB/2020, para 6(i)).

Despite its preventive aims, Section 29A has faced criticism for potentially complicating the resolution process. Critics argue that its "wide scope may exclude genuine resolution applicants," leading to fewer bidders and less competitive insolvency proceedings  (ae7934a7be103b16). As such, while Section 29A serves an essential purpose in maintaining the integrity of insolvency proceedings, its application must balance this with ensuring a fair and competitive process.

This paper argues that the current framework of Section 29A, while necessary, may require refinements to address its broad application and potential to stifle competition. Subsequent sections will explore specific challenges and propose amendments to enhance the effectiveness of Section 29A without undermining its foundational objectives.

## The Supreme Court's Interpretation: A Justifiable Restriction?

Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016 serves as a gatekeeper in the resolution process, disqualifying certain individuals from submitting resolution plans. The provision specifically targets those whose prior conduct or connections with non-performing assets (NPAs) render them unfit to regain control of a debtor company through the resolution process  (85ffa1c2044e403e). Judicial interpretation by the Supreme Court of India has clarified that the disqualification under Section 29A attaches at the time of submission of the resolution plan, ensuring that ineligible parties cannot subvert the process through backdoor entries  (Company Appeal (AT) (Ins) No.121 of 2019, para 15).

The Supreme Court has upheld the constitutional validity of Section 29A, reasoning it does not disturb any vested rights, as resolution applicants do not possess such rights at this stage  (Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. vs Union Of India, 2019, para 2). The provision aims to prevent abuse of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) by those responsible for a debtor's default, thereby reinforcing the integrity of the resolution mechanism  (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, 2016, s. 29A, Paras 1-100).

A compelling counterpoint is provided by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), which has noted exemptions for Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) under certain clauses of Section 29A, suggesting that the rule's rigidity may not be universal  (IA No.192 of 2022 in CP (IB) No.196/BB/2020, para 6(i)). Furthermore, the NCLT has referenced the Supreme Court's interpretation in cases like Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. vs. Union of India, which emphasized the interconnectedness of individuals acting in concert, thereby broadening the scope of disqualification  (IA No.30/2022 IN C.P. (IB)/349(ALD) 2018, para 10).

Despite these clarifications, the application of Section 29A has led to contentious outcomes, such as in the ArcelorMittal case, where parties were deemed ineligible due to NPAs of related entities  (Critical Analysis Of Section 29A – Tranzission). The current legal landscape suggests that while the provision aims to uphold the IBC's objectives, its stringent application may sometimes frustrate potential resolutions that could otherwise benefit the debtor company. On balance, Section 29A's interpretation by the Supreme Court aligns with its intended purpose, but flexibility in its application could enhance its efficacy in complex cases.

## NCLT and NCLAT: Consistency in Application or Divergence?

Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) serves as a crucial provision disqualifying certain entities from submitting resolution plans. It specifically targets wilful defaulters, undischarged insolvents, and promoters of the defaulting company, preventing them from repurchasing their companies at a reduced price, thereby aiming to uphold the integrity of the insolvency process  (85ffa1c2044e403e). 

Judicial interpretation has played a significant role in shaping the application of Section 29A. The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) has demonstrated a strict approach, as seen in its decision to set aside the approval of a resolution plan due to ineligibility under Section 29A, emphasizing that "the Impugned Order approving Resolution Plan is quashed and set aside"  (Company Appeal (AT) (Ins) No.121 of 2019, para 18). The Adjudicating Authority similarly directed the liquidation of a corporate debtor after rejecting a resolution plan, underscoring the importance of adhering to eligibility criteria  (Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) Nos. 253-254, 256-257 & 442-443 of 2024, para 11). 

The rationale behind these decisions lies in the legislative intent to prevent recidivism by defaulters and ensure that only eligible and capable entities manage the corporate debtor's revival  (94cbdd3fb4898439). However, this stringent application has not been free from contention. For instance, the NCLT faced confusion regarding the eligibility of promoters under Section 29A in the MBL Infrastructure case, illustrating the complexities involved in determining ineligibility  (ae7934a7be103b16).

The NCLT and NCLAT have sometimes diverged in their applications of Section 29A. In some instances, the NCLT has rejected claims of eligibility based on technicalities, such as the timing of MSME certificate acquisition  (85fd7e29f15e66a2). The NCLAT's decisions, while aiming for consistency, have occasionally been challenged for their interpretations, necessitating further clarifications.

Currently, the courts continue to grapple with these interpretations, seeking a balance between strict adherence to the statutory disqualifications and the practical realities of insolvency resolution. The inconsistency in the application between NCLT and NCLAT points to a need for clearer guidelines or amendments to ensure uniformity in the application of Section 29A. A more harmonized approach would better serve the objectives of the IBC, providing clarity for stakeholders involved in the insolvency process. While the courts have upheld the provision's intent to protect corporate debtors from their previous management, ongoing debates about its application suggest room for refinement in judicial practice.

## Exceptions and Exemptions: Necessary Flexibility or Loopholes?

Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 outlines the ineligibility criteria for resolution applicants. Notably, it includes specific exemptions for Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs). These exemptions are provided to facilitate the resolution process for these enterprises, which play a crucial role in the economy  (Comp. App. (AT) (Ins) Nos. 253-254, 256-257 & 442-443 of 2024, para 14). The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) has held that Section 240A exempts MSMEs from certain provisions of Section 29A, specifically clauses (c) and (h)  (IA No.192 of 2022 in CP (IB) No.196/BB/2020, para 6(i)).

The rationale behind these exemptions is rooted in the recognition of the unique challenges faced by MSMEs. The legislative intent is to prevent undue hardship to these enterprises while maintaining the integrity of the insolvency process  (94cbdd3fb4898439). However, the application of these exemptions has not been without controversy. In one instance, the NCLT rejected the eligibility of a resolution applicant based on an MSME certificate obtained after the initiation of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP), highlighting the importance of timing in the application of these exemptions  (85fd7e29f15e66a2).

Critics argue that Section 29A's broad definition of connected persons may inadvertently disqualify promoters with genuine intentions to revive their companies, even when they are not directly responsible for the financial distress  (5c095b8760865ee2). Recommendations have been made to refine these definitions to prevent over-deterrence and to allow exceptions for genuine promoters who can demonstrate that their non-performing assets (NPAs) were due to external factors  (5c095b8760865ee2).

Currently, the law maintains a delicate balance between preventing undesirable applicants from taking over corporate debtors and allowing genuine promoters an opportunity to revive their businesses. The exemptions for MSMEs represent a necessary flexibility within Section 29A, though the broad definitions and strict application criteria may still pose challenges. A compelling argument can be made for further refinement of these provisions to better align with their intended purpose without creating undue loopholes.

On balance, while the exemptions under Section 29A provide necessary flexibility, the potential for misinterpretation and misuse suggests the need for ongoing evaluation and adjustment to ensure that the objectives of the IBC are met effectively.

## Impact on Resolution Applicants: A Deterrent or a Filter?

Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) imposes stringent eligibility criteria on resolution applicants, primarily targeting those with past financial mismanagement or connections to non-performing assets  (5c095b8760865ee2). The provision's statutory rule is clear: it disqualifies individuals responsible for a company's financial distress, aiming to prevent them from regaining control at discounted prices  (Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. vs Union Of India, 2019, para 5). This rule applies at the time of submission of the resolution plan  (Company Appeal (AT) (Ins) No.121 of 2019, para 15), ensuring that only eligible applicants participate in the process.

Judicial interpretation reaffirms the statutory rule, emphasizing that resolution applicants have no vested right to be considered in the process  (Company Appeal (AT) (Ins) No.121 of 2019, para 12). Courts have upheld the provision's validity, underscoring its role in maintaining the integrity of the resolution process by excluding undesirable applicants  (Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. vs Union Of India, 2019, para 2). The rationale behind Section 29A is to ensure that those who contributed to a corporate debtor's financial distress do not benefit from the insolvency process  (Critical Analysis Of Section 29A – Tranzission).

However, there is criticism regarding the provision's broad application. It is argued that Section 29A complicates the resolution process, leading to extensive litigation and potentially hindering innocent applicants due to technicalities  (Section 29A of the Insolvency Code: A Critique of its Impact & Recent Developments – IndiaCorpLaw). This has resulted in a reduction of competition among resolution applicants, which could detract from achieving the IBC's objective of maximizing asset value  (Section 29A of the Insolvency Code: A Critique of its Impact & Recent Developments – IndiaCorpLaw).

Despite these critiques, current legal interpretation remains firm: Section 29A is not retroactive and applies only to resolution plans submitted after its enactment  (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, 2016, s. 29A, Paras 50-75). The provision continues to act as a filter, ensuring that only those without a history of financial mismanagement can lead the revival of the corporate debtor.

The impact of Section 29A, therefore, hinges on its ability to balance the exclusion of culpable parties with the need to attract genuine resolution applicants. While it has successfully deterred undesirable participants, it also risks deterring potential applicants who may be unfairly caught by its broad criteria. On balance, the provision serves as a necessary filter, but its application could be refined to better align with the IBC's objectives of revival and continuity of the corporate debtor  (94cbdd3fb4898439).

## Amendments and Proposed Changes: Addressing the Criticisms?

Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) has been the subject of significant scrutiny and proposed reforms. The provision primarily aims to bar certain individuals and entities from participating in the insolvency resolution process. Specifically, it seeks to prevent those who have contributed to the financial distress of the corporate debtor from regaining control through the resolution process  (94cbdd3fb4898439). The legislative amendments and proposed changes aim to refine these eligibility criteria and address criticisms regarding its broad application.

Recent amendments to the IBC Regulations, as of January 2020, have reinforced the ineligibility provisions under Section 29A by disallowing ineligible persons from participating in compromises or arrangements under Section 230  (94cbdd3fb4898439). This amendment aligns with the legislative intent to prevent ex-promoters who may have contributed to a company's financial decline from regaining control  (94cbdd3fb4898439).

Despite these amendments, criticisms persist. The provisions are viewed as overly broad, potentially disqualifying individuals who may not have directly contributed to the financial issues of the debtor company. Recommendations have been made to introduce exceptions for genuine promoters whose financial distress was caused by external factors beyond their control  (5c095b8760865ee2). Additionally, the Insolvency Law Committee has suggested narrowing the scope of Section 29A to exclude pure play financial entities and to refine clauses related to criminal convictions and director disqualifications  (Section 29A of the Insolvency Code: A Critique of its Impact & Recent Developments – IndiaCorpLaw),  (Section 29A of the Insolvency Code: A Critique of its Impact & Recent Developments – IndiaCorpLaw).

Currently, the law seeks to balance preventing undesirable entities from regaining control while avoiding undue disqualifications. The Cabinet's approval of a new Ordinance based on the Committee's recommendations indicates a move towards refining these provisions  (Section 29A of the Insolvency Code: A Critique of its Impact & Recent Developments – IndiaCorpLaw). However, the challenge remains in ensuring that these amendments do not inadvertently prolong the resolution process, a concern noted by the Committee  (Section 29A of the Insolvency Code: A Critique of its Impact & Recent Developments – IndiaCorpLaw). 

On balance, while the amendments and proposed changes address some criticisms, the effectiveness of these measures will depend on their implementation and the ability to distinguish between culpable and non-culpable parties within the insolvency framework. This nuanced approach is crucial for maintaining the integrity and efficiency of the insolvency resolution process.

## The Case Against Section 29A: Criticisms and Counterarguments

Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) was introduced with the intent to prevent promoters who are responsible for non-performing assets (NPAs) from benefiting from the insolvency process. The legislative intent, as discussed by the NCLAT, is to ensure that "promoters who are themselves responsible for the NPAs should not be allowed to take advantage of the process under IBC"  (Company Appeal (AT) (Ins) No.121 of 2019, para 10). The provision aims to curb the practice of defaulting promoters acquiring assets at discounted prices, which would defeat the purpose of the IBC by allowing them a backdoor entry  (ae7934a7be103b16).

However, this broad prohibition has not been without criticism. It has been argued that Section 29A's wide scope leads to the exclusion of many genuine resolution applicants due to undefined terms like 'related to', which can inadvertently disqualify promoters with a genuine interest in reviving the company, even if they were not directly responsible for the financial distress  (5c095b8760865ee2). Moreover, the section has been criticized for complicating the resolution process and leading to extensive litigation, thereby impeding successful time-bound resolutions  (Section 29A of the Insolvency Code: A Critique of its Impact & Recent Developments – IndiaCorpLaw).

Recommendations have been made for a more nuanced application of Section 29A, including clearer definitions and exceptions for genuine promoters who can demonstrate external factors beyond their control  (5c095b8760865ee2). There is also a call for leniency by the courts in interpreting disqualifications to maximize the objectives of the IBC, such as maximizing asset value and promoting entrepreneurship, while ensuring that the resolution process is not hijacked by defaulting promoters  (ae7934a7be103b16).

In conclusion, while Section 29A serves an important purpose in preventing abuse of the insolvency process, its current application may not align perfectly with its intended goals. The better view is to refine the provision's criteria and definitions to ensure a balance between preventing exploitation by defaulters and allowing genuine resolution efforts to proceed. This balance is crucial to protect both the resolution process and stakeholder interests  (IJLLR Journal, Mar 27, 2025).

## Conclusion: Navigating the Future of Section 29A

Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) has been subject to significant judicial scrutiny and legislative amendments. The Supreme Court upheld its constitutional validity, emphasizing that it aims to "prevent individuals who contributed to a company’s financial distress from benefitting from the insolvency process"  (IA No.30/2022 IN C.P. (IB)/349(ALD) 2018, para 18). Notably, Section 29A(c) disqualifies resolution applicants associated with non-performing assets, and its applicability is prospective, not retrospective  (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, 2016, s. 29A, Paras 50-75).

Critics argue that Section 29A complicates the resolution process, leading to extensive litigation and potentially excluding genuine applicants due to its broad scope  (Section 29A of the Insolvency Code: A Critique of its Impact & Recent Developments – IndiaCorpLaw). The NCLT and NCLAT have faced challenges in applying these provisions, particularly concerning the definition of 'related party' and the eligibility of promoters  (ae7934a7be103b16). Nevertheless, amendments have introduced some clarity and exceptions, especially for MSMEs, reflecting an effort to balance the interests of various stakeholders  (b8aa49aca613960d).

On balance, Section 29A plays a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the resolution process by disqualifying undesirable applicants. However, its broad definitions and stringent criteria necessitate a more nuanced application to avoid hindering legitimate resolutions. Future reforms should focus on refining these definitions and considering exceptions for genuine cases, aligning the provision with its intended purpose while minimizing unintended exclusions.
